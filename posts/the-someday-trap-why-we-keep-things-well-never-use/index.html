The Someday Trap: Why We Keep Things We'll Never Use

2026-06-14 · Simple Living · Tags: minimalistlifepro, simple living

We all have them—those items that sit quietly in the back of a closet, the bottom of a drawer, or the highest shelf in the garage. The bread maker that was going to transform our breakfasts. The sewing machine we were certain we'd learn to use. The stack of art supplies for the creative hobby we'd take up "someday." These objects aren't just clutter; they're monuments to future selves we keep waiting to become. The problem is that "someday" rarely arrives, and in the meantime, these items occupy more than physical space—they occupy mental space, emotional energy, and a surprising amount of our present lives. In this article, we'll explore the psychology behind the someday trap, why we cling to these unused possessions, and—most importantly—how to free ourselves from the weight of all those good intentions.

The Psychology of the Someday Self

When we hold onto items for a future we imagine, we're not really holding onto the objects themselves. We're holding onto an identity. The person who will bake sourdough from scratch, who will paint watercolors on Sunday mornings, who will host elegant dinner parties with hand-written place cards—that person is aspirational. And there's nothing wrong with aspiration. The trouble begins when these aspirational objects become permanent residents in our homes, silently reminding us of who we're not yet.

Psychologists call this the "possible self"—the version of ourselves we hope to become. It's a powerful motivator, but it can also be a trap. When we buy or keep things for this possible self, we're essentially paying rent on an identity we haven't moved into yet. The yoga mat in the corner isn't just rubber and foam; it's a daily reminder that you haven't been to class in months. The French cookbook isn't just paper and ink; it's a judgment on your takeout habit.

Recognizing this pattern is the first step toward breaking it. Ask yourself: Am I keeping this because I genuinely use it, or because I want to be the kind of person who would use it? The answer can be uncomfortable, but it's also liberating. Once you see the gap between your aspirational self and your actual self, you can make conscious choices about which future you want to invest in—and which ones you're ready to let go.

The Hidden Cost of Holding On

We tend to think of clutter as a purely physical problem—too much stuff in too little space. But the cost of the someday trap goes far beyond square footage. Every item you keep for a future that hasn't arrived is an item that demands attention, even if you don't consciously realize it. Your brain registers the pile of unread books on the nightstand. It notices the unused exercise equipment in the corner of the bedroom. It catalogs the craft supplies in the hall closet.

This mental load is real. Researchers have found that physical clutter competes for your attention, making it harder to focus and increasing feelings of stress and anxiety. When you're surrounded by unfinished projects and unused tools, your brain is constantly processing a low-grade sense of failure. It's the background hum of all those good intentions gone dormant.

There's also a financial cost to consider. The money you spent on these items is already gone—that's a sunk cost, and it's not coming back. But there's an ongoing cost in the form of storage. You're paying for the space these items occupy, whether that's in a larger apartment, a storage unit, or simply the lost potential of a room that could serve a purpose you actually need. Every square foot devoted to someday is a square foot stolen from today.

The One-Year Rule: A Practical Test

So how do you break free from the someday trap? One of the most effective strategies I've found is what I call the One-Year Rule. It's simple: if you haven't used an item in the past year, and you don't have a concrete, scheduled plan to use it in the next year, it's time to let it go. This rule works because it forces you to be honest about your intentions. "Someday" isn't a plan; it's a fantasy. A date on the calendar is a plan.

But here's the key: the One-Year Rule isn't about being harsh with yourself. It's about being realistic. If you genuinely want to learn to play the guitar, that's wonderful—but are you actually going to start lessons next month, or have you been saying that for three years? If the answer is the latter, the guitar isn't serving your future self; it's serving your guilt. Letting it go doesn't mean giving up on your dream. It means freeing yourself from the weight of an unused instrument so you can either commit to the dream fully or release it with grace.

For items that carry sentimental weight or significant financial value, you can modify the rule. The point isn't to create a rigid system; it's to create a framework for honest assessment. When you pick up an item and feel that familiar tug of "maybe someday," pause and ask: What would it take for me to use this in the next year? If you can't name a specific action, a specific date, or a specific reason, you have your answer.

From Someday to Today: Making Space for What Matters

When you release the someday items, something remarkable happens: you create space—not just in your home, but in your life. The physical space that opens up is obvious, but the mental space is even more valuable. Without the constant background hum of unused possessions, you'll find it easier to focus on what's actually in front of you. The present moment becomes less cluttered, and with that clarity comes a new kind of energy.

This is the moment to ask yourself a different question: What do I want my life to look like right now, not someday? Maybe you want to cook more, but you don't need a bread maker to do it—you need a simple skillet and a good recipe. Maybe you want to be more creative, but you don't need a studio full of supplies—you need a sketchbook and a pen. The tools you actually use don't have to be elaborate or expensive. They just have to be present.

I've found that when I let go of the someday items, I naturally become more engaged with the things I already have. The books I kept are the ones I actually read. The kitchen tools I kept are the ones I actually use. My home becomes a reflection of my real life, not my fantasy life—and that's a far more peaceful place to be.

Letting Go with Gratitude, Not Guilt

The hardest part of releasing someday items is often the emotional weight. These objects represent hopes and dreams, and letting them go can feel like admitting defeat. But I want to offer a different perspective: letting go isn't failure; it's growth. The person who bought that bread maker was dreaming of homemade bread, and that dream was valid. But you're not the same person you were then. You've changed, your circumstances have changed, and your dreams have evolved.

Instead of guilt, try gratitude. Thank the item for what it represented—the hope, the intention, the possibility. Then release it with the understanding that you're making room for something new. You're not giving up on your future; you're making space for a future that's more aligned with who you actually are.

When you donate or sell these items, you're also giving them a second life. Someone else might use that bread maker every single week. That sewing machine might become the centerpiece of someone's creative practice. By letting go, you're not discarding value; you're redistributing it. And in the process, you're freeing yourself from the weight of all those unused possibilities.

Building a Home for Your Real Life

Once you've cleared out the someday items, the final step is to be intentional about what you allow back in. This is where the real work of minimalism begins. Before you bring anything new into your home, ask yourself: Will I use this in the next month? Does it serve my life as it is right now, or am I buying it for a future self that may never arrive? These questions aren't meant to be restrictive; they're meant to be clarifying.

I've also found it helpful to create a "someday list"—not a list of things to buy, but a list of things to do. When I feel the urge to acquire something for a future activity, I write down the activity instead. This gives me the satisfaction of acknowledging the aspiration without the burden of the object. If the activity becomes a real priority, I can pursue it then—and I'll know exactly what I need when the time comes.

Your home should be a reflection of your present life, not a museum of your unrealized plans. When you walk through your front door, you should feel a sense of relief, not a sense of obligation. You should see the things you love and use, not the things you're avoiding. This is the quiet power of letting go: it doesn't take anything away from you. It gives you back the space, the time, and the energy to live the life you have, right now, instead of waiting for a someday that may never come.

Conclusion

The someday trap is one of the most subtle and persistent forms of clutter. It's not about hoarding or collecting; it's about hope. We keep these items because we want to believe in our future selves, and that's a beautiful impulse. But when hope becomes a burden, it's time to reexamine our choices. By letting go of the things we'll never use, we're not abandoning our dreams—we're making room for the life we're actually living. We're choosing presence over potential, today over someday. And in that choice, we find a deeper kind of peace: the peace of a home that holds only what we truly need, and a heart that's free to embrace what's real.
